Childhood Cancer Awareness

Understanding the challenges and supporting affected families

Introduction & Awareness

Childhood Cancer Awareness Month is observed annually in September to increase exposure to the community about challenges faced by children with cancer, bring awareness for research into cures and treatments, and support affected families.

🌍

400,000

children aged 0-19 diagnosed with cancer annually

- World Health Organisation (WHO)

The idea of raising awareness on Childhood Cancer started in 1990 when former American President George Bush introduced the concept, but it wasn't officially recognized until 26th September 2019. Afterwards, organizations around the globe have come together each September to raise awareness by displaying gold ribbons and hosting events that shine a light on childhood cancer. Childhood Cancer Awareness Month is established through the combined efforts of various organizations and governmental bodies worldwide, along with significant momentum provided by former US President Barack Obama since 2012.

🎗️

The golden ribbon represents that children are truly a treasure for our community, delivering us a reminder that we must do our best to protect them. This golden ribbon represents all types of childhood cancers to raise awareness about this issue in our community.

Research published on the National Institutes of Health's website has shown that cancer will not just affect and disrupt the children's mental and emotional health, it will also worsen the relationship and mental health of their family members. An assessment that involved 174 patients and 149 siblings was performed, while the Actor Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) was used to estimate their relationship and mental status. It was proven that families that have children with cancer have a lower score compared to normal families, although they are still very supportive of the patient.

Understanding Childhood Cancer

According to a study published on MedlinePlus, childhood cancer often occurs due to genetic inheritance and genetic changes during their growth, but most of the time the cause of their cancer is not known. However, not all children with the variant gene will get cancer. Children born with Down syndrome are also more likely to develop leukaemia. This makes a difference between childhood and adult cancer, as they don't develop because of unhealthy lifestyle choices or habits, like an imbalanced diet and smoking. But the good news is that childhood cancers hold a high chance of recovery if they are diagnosed early and treated with proper medication.

Common Affected Areas

When childhood cancers appear, they often affect blood cells, the lymph system, the brain, the adrenal gland, and the bones.

Treatment Options

Treated similarly to adult cancer, including chemotherapy, radiation therapy, immunotherapy, surgery, and stem cell transplant.

But for children, the frequency of therapy, type of medicine, and need for surgery might differ from adults, as cancer cells in children usually respond better to treatments compared to adult cancers. Studies show that children tend to handle a higher dosage of chemotherapy in short periods before side effects occur.

However, side effects might still happen while undergoing cancer treatment. These involve:

  • • Infection
  • • Poor appetite
  • • Hair loss
  • • Constipation
  • • Diarrhoea
  • • Infertility
  • • Mouth sores
  • • Hearing damage

Cancer treatments may also delay their body growth and even form another cancer as time progresses. These side effects are often noticed after weeks or years of treatment; this situation is widely recognised as 'late effects.'
